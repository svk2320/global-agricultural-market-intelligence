# Business Insights Report

**Project:** Global Agricultural Market Intelligence Platform
**Scope:** 2001–2020, global country-level analysis
**Data sources:** FAOSTAT agricultural production, trade, food security, and commodity price indicators

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Key findings from the Global Agricultural Market Intelligence Platform (2001–2020):

- **Global food security improved on average** — the population-weighted food supply adequacy indicator rose from 1.49 (2001) to 1.64 (2020) — but this masks real deterioration in specific countries: **Syria, Venezuela, Egypt, and Nigeria** show the steepest declines and are the clearest "at risk" signals for food-assistance targeting.
- **Wheat yield gains are highly uneven across countries**: Iraq (+298%) and Kuwait (+193%) posted the largest 2001–2020 gains, while Taiwan, Lesotho, and New Caledonia lost 40–70% of their 2001 yield — a pattern that appears to be influenced by both agricultural inputs and broader economic and political conditions.
- **Fertilizer prices rose faster than any other tracked commodity index** (+114% globally, 2001–2020), which has direct implications for which countries can afford input-intensive productivity improvements.
- **Trade dependency shows two distinct patterns**: countries vary widely in how import-dependent they are on agrochemical inputs, and separately, many smaller exporters are highly concentrated in a single export market — both are actionable diversification signals.
- **Wheat price volatility peaked in 2010 (11.8%)** and during the 2007–2008 global food price crisis (9.0–9.7%), directly relevant to buy/sell and export timing decisions.

## 3. Food Security Insights

**Business question:** Which countries are becoming food insecure?

### Global Trend

The global median food supply adequacy level rose steadily from **1.49 in 2001 to 1.61 in 2020**. The population-weighted view — which reflects the average *person* worldwide rather than the average *country* — tells a similar story, rising from **1.49 to 1.64** over the same period. A value above 1.0 means average food energy supply exceeds the minimum requirement for a healthy population.

The number of countries falling below this minimum threshold **fell from 2 in 2001 to 0 in 2020**, staying at 0–1 for most years after 2014.

### Countries at Risk

Despite the positive global trend, several countries show clear deterioration between 2001 and 2020:

| Rank | Country | 2001 | 2020 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Syrian Arab Republic | 1.549 | 1.340 | −0.209 |
| 2 | Venezuela | 1.418 | 1.267 | −0.151 |
| 3 | Egypt | 1.824 | 1.716 | −0.107 |
| 4 | Nigeria | 1.555 | 1.451 | −0.103 |
| 5 | Malta | 1.780 | 1.679 | −0.102 |
| 6 | Iran | 1.672 | 1.575 | −0.098 |
| 7 | Democratic Republic of the Congo | 1.362 | 1.264 | −0.097 |
| 8 | Libya | 1.698 | 1.602 | −0.096 |
| 9 | South Africa | 1.587 | 1.498 | −0.090 |
| 10 | Madagascar | 1.217 | 1.140 | −0.077 |

### Key Interpretation

This ranking directly supports the platform's core food-security decisions: which countries need food assistance, and which are at risk next year. Several of the largest decliners (Syria, Venezuela, Libya) are countries with documented conflict or severe economic crisis over this period, which lends credibility to the trend. Notably, Egypt and Nigeria appear on this list despite still having comfortable absolute food-security levels — a reminder that **trend direction matters as much as absolute level** when anticipating future risk.

The broad-based nature of the global improvement (both the median country and the average person show gains) suggests the gains are not concentrated only in a handful of very populous countries.

## 4. Agricultural Productivity Insights

**Business question:** Why are some countries producing more than others?

### Yield Trends

Global median wheat yield rose from **2,333 (index units) in 2001 to 2,955 in 2020** — roughly a 27% increase — with a visible dip in 2018 before recovering by 2020. This is consistent with decades of broad agronomic improvement (seed genetics, mechanization, wider fertilizer access), but the global median obscures very large differences between individual countries.

Fertilizer and pesticide use per hectare of cropland both show wide variation across countries: a small number of high-input agricultural systems use dramatically more fertilizer and pesticide per hectare than the typical country, while the majority operate at much lower input intensity.

### Productivity Leaders and Laggards

The largest wheat yield gains between 2001 and 2020 were concentrated in:

| Top Improving | 2001 | 2020 | % Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iraq | 741 | 2,945 | +297.6% |
| Kuwait | 2,391 | 7,000 | +192.8% |
| Uruguay | 1,149 | 3,269 | +184.5% |
| Estonia | 2,230 | 5,002 | +124.3% |
| Mali | 2,524 | 5,303 | +110.1% |

The largest declines were concentrated in:

| Top Declining | 2001 | 2020 | % Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| New Caledonia | 1,850 | 557 | −69.9% |
| Lesotho | 2,052 | 697 | −66.0% |
| Angola | 1,667 | 670 | −59.8% |
| DPR Korea | 2,163 | 1,248 | −42.3% |
| Taiwan | 3,441 | 2,000 | −41.9% |

### Key Interpretation

The top improvers include countries with clear policy-driven modernization stories — Iraq's post-conflict agricultural investment, Kuwait's targeted irrigation and greenhouse programs given its arid climate, and Baltic states benefiting from EU accession-era agricultural modernization (Estonia, alongside Latvia and Lithuania). The top decliners are largely conflict-affected or resource-constrained economies (Lesotho, Angola, DPRK), suggesting that yield trends appear to be influenced by both agricultural inputs and broader economic and political conditions, rather than input levels alone.

This points toward two distinct levers for the platform's stated decisions: **input investment** (fertilizer, irrigation, technology) clearly matters, but **stability and continuity of agricultural policy** appears to be an equally important driver of long-run productivity gains.

## 5. Trade Intelligence Insights

**Business question:** How dependent is each country on imports and exports?

### Import Dependency

Countries vary widely in how dependent they are on imports for key agricultural inputs. Some countries — such as Albania, at 91–100% import-dependent across most years — rely almost entirely on external suppliers for the inputs they need, making them highly exposed to global supply disruptions.

The countries whose import dependency increased most sharply between 2001 and 2020:

| Country | 2001 | 2020 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominica | 16.3% | 100.0% | +83.7 pp |
| Niger | 31.4% | 95.4% | +63.9 pp |
| Venezuela | 47.6% | 95.0% | +47.4 pp |
| American Samoa | 53.9% | 99.1% | +45.2 pp |
| Western Sahara | 30.2% | 70.3% | +40.0 pp |

### Export Concentration

A number of smaller exporters are highly concentrated in a single export market — for example, several small island and developing economies export the majority of a given commodity to just one trading partner. Similarly, many countries source a significant share of their imports from only one or two "significant" partners, rather than a diversified base of suppliers.

### Key Interpretation

Countries approaching 100% import dependency rely heavily on external suppliers for that commodity category and may face higher exposure to supply disruptions — worth flagging for policymakers considering domestic-capacity investment or supplier diversification.

Export and import concentration findings directly support the platform's "diversify suppliers" and "find new export markets" decisions: countries with a single dominant trading partner face outsized exposure if that relationship is disrupted, whether by policy change, conflict, or economic shock.

## 6. Commodity Market Insights

**Business question:** Why are agricultural commodity prices changing?

### Price Volatility

Annual wheat price volatility ranged from **2.8% in 2001 to a peak of 11.8% in 2010**, with additional spikes during 2007–2008 (9.0–9.7%). These spikes align with well-documented global food price crisis periods, driven by drought-related supply shocks, export restrictions, and rising input costs (including the fertilizer price surge described below).

### Inflation Impact

From 2001 to 2020, global agricultural commodity indices rose sharply:

| Index | 2001 | 2020 | % Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fertilizers | 34.9 | 74.6 | +114.1% |
| Food | 48.7 | 93.1 | +91.1% |
| Agriculture (overall) | 47.1 | 87.1 | +85.0% |

At the country level, food inflation varies substantially relative to this global benchmark. A small number of countries experiencing severe macroeconomic crises show extreme divergence from the global trend, while several African and Middle Eastern economies show more moderate but still elevated food-inflation gaps of 40+ percentage points above the global benchmark in the years examined.

Currency movements also play a meaningful role in local commodity price changes: countries with currencies pegged to the US dollar show essentially zero currency-driven price exposure, while countries experiencing currency instability show much larger swings in local commodity prices independent of the underlying global commodity price itself.

### Key Interpretation

**Fertilizer prices rose faster than food or overall agriculture prices** over this period — a finding directly relevant to the Productivity insights above: if input costs are rising faster than output prices, that constrains which countries can afford to pursue input-intensive yield improvements, potentially widening the productivity gap between wealthier and resource-constrained producing nations.

The 2007–2008 and 2010 volatility spikes, together with the sustained rise in the fertilizer index, directly support the platform's "buy now or later," "export now or later," and government price-intervention decisions.

## 7. Business Recommendations

Based on the findings above:

1. **Prioritize food-assistance monitoring for Syria, Venezuela, Egypt, and Nigeria** — these countries show the clearest downward trend in food security over the analysis period, even where absolute levels remain above the minimum threshold.
2. **Target productivity investment toward stability, not just inputs** — the clearest yield gains occurred in countries undergoing agricultural modernization or post-conflict recovery, suggesting that policy stability and targeted infrastructure investment (irrigation, mechanization) may deliver returns as large as fertilizer/pesticide access alone.
3. **Flag single-partner trade exposure as a diversification priority** — countries with one dominant export or import partner should be prioritized for supplier/market diversification support, given their outsized exposure to a single relationship being disrupted.
4. **Treat fertilizer cost trends as an early-warning input for productivity policy** — since fertilizer prices have outpaced food and agriculture prices overall, rising input costs are a leading risk factor for producers in lower-income countries specifically.
5. **Use 2007–2008 and 2010 as reference case studies** for stress-testing buy/sell and price-intervention strategies, since these periods represent the clearest historical examples of extreme commodity price volatility in the analysis window.

## 8. Limitations

- **Food security** is measured using a dietary energy adequacy proxy rather than a direct, universally available hunger indicator, since no single indicator offers both direct measurement and full 2001–2020 country coverage. This proxy reflects national average supply and does not capture within-country distributional inequality — a country can show a comfortable national average while specific populations remain food insecure.
- **Yield, trend, and dependency rankings** are based on a two-point comparison (2001 vs. 2020) rather than a full trend analysis across all 20 years. This is a simpler, more interpretable approach but is more sensitive to an unusual value in either endpoint year than a full trend-line method would be.
- **Trade dependency findings cover two related but distinct commodity groups** (agrochemical inputs for import dependency, and a processed food category for export/import concentration), reflecting differences in what each underlying dataset covers — not a limitation in interpretation, but worth noting when comparing these findings directly against one another.
- **Extreme country-level outliers** (most notably countries experiencing hyperinflation or currency collapse) can distort simple averages in the commodity price findings; the individual country tables above should be read alongside the global benchmark rather than as a single blended average.
- **Productivity findings are based on a single representative crop (wheat)** rather than an all-crop composite, chosen for its full 2001–2020 country coverage; findings may differ for other crops or livestock categories.
